# Worked generator–critic examples

All examples below are fictional teaching fixtures. They are not evidence about any
real product. Apply the rule, not the group names or a fixed number of profiles.

## 1. Preparing children for school: personal or organizational practice?

**Input:** "I prepare children for first grade in an office. We use games."
**Generator v0:** Teachers → Private school-preparation tutors.
**Critic:** G2/E2. An office and first-grade preparation do not establish independent
practice. A hired teacher at a center would give the same answer. Wrong placement would
suggest an individual buying path without grounds. Ask who recruits learners, organizes
the program, selects the platform and pays.
**Generator response:** Accept. Preserve "School-preparation teaching" as a candidate
with unknown organizational scope; retain games as the stated criterion/context.
**Lead:** Defer placement, preserve the case. A later statement "I recruit my own pupils
and select my tools" supports the tutor profile. "I manage a center and choose a shared
system for our teachers" supports an organizational profile. Payer still needs evidence.

## 2. The same name hides two kinds of course author

**Input:** A language tutor launched a four-week program alongside lessons for their
own pupils. Another person sells drawing courses and gives no lessons.
**Generator v0:** Language tutors → Course and marathon authors; include both.
**Critic:** C1/C3. The drawing-course author fails the parent's boundary. The title
also claims more than the tutor case. Preserve the narrower practice and compare
the independent-course business as a separate candidate.
**Generator response:** Rename child "Language tutors with their own courses" and
include only the first case. Its program structure may change enrollment and progress
tracking; those effects remain hypotheses. Keep the drawing-course business separate.
**Lead:** Accept the narrowed child and independent candidate. No inferred revenue or
self-paced delivery. A plan to launch a course would not prove an existing launch.

## 3. Ready-made materials: characteristic or meaningful subsegment?

**Input:** A tutor buys a worksheet occasionally. Another teacher organizes their
teaching around a purchased course and keeps its platform to retain access.
**Generator v0:** A universal segment "Buyers of ready materials" across all teachers.
**Critic:** S2/S3/C2. Occasional purchase and dependence on a course describe different
practices. What distinct product decision follows for each? Shared purchase alone is
not enough, and the second person's employment is unknown.
**Generator response:** Keep occasional purchase as context. Propose "Teachers building
their program around purchased courses" as a candidate, with access/portability as
possible selection criteria. Parent placement and broader recurrence remain unknown.
**Lead:** Accept candidate status, not a proven universal segment. No automatic children
under every teaching profile, no claim of our platform earning these purchases.

## 4. The respondent is a teacher; the practice is something else

**Input:** A teacher mainly makes exercises for their own children. An assistant makes
lessons for another teacher and is paid for that labor. A third teacher sells worksheets
to colleagues. Our platform payment is not mentioned in any case.
**Generator v0:** Put everyone under private teachers and mark them as paying customers.
**Critic:** G2/S1/E2. Teaching one's children, commissioned production and publishing
materials have different beneficiaries. The assistant's fee is not platform payment.
Nor does that commission establish who selected the software: the teacher may set
the lesson brief while the assistant picks an authoring tool. Both are different decisions.
**Generator response:** Describe family learning, commissioned lesson production and
distribution to colleagues as separate practices. Propose familiar navigation families
such as "Family learning" and "Learning-material creators" if useful for this map.
**Lead:** Accept the practice distinctions. These are possible navigation choices, not
mandatory groups for all education products. Family use does not prove a parent market.
Leave platform chooser unknown where only a lesson commissioner is named.

## 5. Employment does not establish institutional adoption

**Input:** "My language school requires lesson reports. For my private pupils I use
this platform; at school we use a different one."
**Generator v0:** Schools → Language schools using our platform; payer is employer.
**Critic:** S4/E2. Our product is only observed in private practice. The school is an
employment/discovery context; both institutional adoption and payer are unsupported.
**Generator response:** Retain two practice contexts, bind our usage to private teaching.
Keep an employed-teacher candidate with reporting as observed context and other value
differences as hypotheses. Leave the payer unknown.
**Lead:** Accept the correction; no transfer of evidence from one context to the other.

## 6. A complaint becomes an unrecognizable segment

**Input:** Several forms mention too much checking. Some are class teachers, others
are private tutors; sources do not compare relative priorities.
**Generator v0:** Segment "Teachers with heavy checking load", child "Online teachers".
**Critic:** S1/S2/C1/E3. Neither title identifies a coherent practice or a useful nested
boundary. Online does not imply heavy checking. Complaint frequency is not priority.
**Generator response:** Examine class teachers and tutors as recognizable practices.
Checking is a job/criterion within each. Keep any proposed difference in volume,
workflow and required feedback explicit as an inference until sourced.
**Lead:** Retire the old labels with recorded reasons, preserve their evidence. No
automatic split by pain score, pupil-count threshold or online/offline combination.

## 7. No respondents: critic must not erase a coherent hypothesis

**Input:** FieldBoard offers scheduling and shared checklists (team claims); no cases.
Possible customers: independent repair specialists and property management companies.
**Generator v0:** Two recognizable profiles: solo specialists organizing their own
visits; property managers coordinating work across several properties and contractors.
All jobs, buying roles and criteria are hypotheses; payment is unknown.
**Critic:** S5/E1. No demand is proven, but zero cases is not grounds to reject the map.
Challenge instead whether shared history and dispatch explain a different decision,
and whether FieldBoard can actually support multi-party access.
**Generator response:** Clarify the organizational practice and keep permissions and
dispatch capability as unknown; the proposed fit is conditional. Keep a recruitment
question about coordination across contractors.
**Lead:** Accept two hypotheses for testing, with fit limitations. Do not copy Teachers,
Schools or four mandatory groups from other examples.

## 8. Generator can rebut a critic; consensus is not required

**Input:** One anonymous form directly describes a material creator working to a
teacher's specification. Another nearly identical form has unknown identity.
**Critic:** Merge into "Teachers"; two examples are too few for a separate candidate.
**Generator response:** Reject that objection under S1/S5/E3. The beneficiary and
commissioned production are explicit; a minimum count is not the rule. Retain a
candidate while keeping two materials and unknown unique-person count.
**Lead:** Uphold the rebuttal. Require the critic to reassess the actual boundary/value
difference, not demand more respondents as a condition for admitting any hypothesis.
Record the rejected objection and rationale. A credible objection about missing
platform usage can still stand independently.
